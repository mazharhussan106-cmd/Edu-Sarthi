
@REQ-015
topic: 03 Requirements
level: Intermediate
term: Assumptions and risks
cue: How do you record what you are guessing and what could go wrong?
category: Technique
frequency: High
importance: 5
origin: Plans rest on things nobody has checked yet, and unwritten assumptions turn into surprises at the worst moment.
pro_tip: Give each assumption an owner and a date by which it will be checked.
memory_story: Assumptions are the loose planks in a bridge, and a risk list tells you where to test them.
visual: A table with assumption, owner, check date and a risk rating.
emotion: The calm of surprises named early.
definition: An assumption is something treated as true without proof, and a risk is an uncertain event that could harm the project, and both are recorded with an owner and a plan.
explanation: Typical assumptions are that data will be available by a date, that test accounts exist or that an API can handle the load. Risks are rated by likelihood and impact, with an action to reduce them. Review the list at every status meeting. An assumption that turns out false becomes an issue and may trigger a change request.
simple_explain: Write down what you are guessing and what could go wrong, and check them early.
analogy: Assumptions are the loose planks in a bridge, and a risk list tells you where to test them.
formula_rule: Assumption, owner, check date; risk, likelihood, impact, action.
example_1: Assuming the customer will provide sample data by Friday.
example_2: A risk that the security review takes longer than planned.
example_3: An assumption that the existing API allows bulk export.
related: scoping, estimating, change requests, status updates
word_family: assumption, risk, issue, likelihood, mitigation
where_used: Proposals | Planning | Status meetings
mini_conversation: A: We assumed the data would be clean. | B: That should have been on the risk list with a check date.
misconception: ✗ Risks are only worth listing if they are likely → ✓ High-impact risks matter even when unlikely
contrast: Risk vs issue: something that might happen versus something that already has.
avoid: Do not leave assumptions unwritten and unchecked.
memory_trick: Name it, own it, check it early.
real_project: Checking a data assumption in week one prevented a month of rework.
real_interview: An interviewer may ask: How do you manage project risks?
real_daily: It is like checking the weather forecast before a trip.
mcq_question: What should each assumption have?
mcq_options: A) A colour B) A price C) A nickname D) An owner and a check date
mcq_answer: D
mcq_why: Without an owner and date, assumptions are never verified.
fill_blank: A risk that has already happened becomes an ____ → issue
apply: A plan relies on data arriving Friday. What do you record? → An assumption with an owner and a check date
explain_task: Explain the difference between an assumption, a risk and an issue.
own_task: Write five assumptions and two risks for an integration project.

@REQ-016
topic: 03 Requirements
level: Intermediate
term: Change requests
cue: How do you handle new asks after the scope is agreed?
category: Process
frequency: High
importance: 4
origin: Requirements always change, so a light process is needed to judge each change on cost, time and value.
pro_tip: Treat a change as a normal event, and respond with options rather than a flat refusal.
memory_story: A change request is a toll gate where new work is weighed before entering the road.
visual: A form with change, reason, effort, impact and decision.
emotion: The fairness of a transparent trade-off.
definition: A change request is a recorded proposal to alter agreed scope, which is assessed for effort, risk and value, then approved, rejected or scheduled.
explanation: Record what is asked, why, who asked and the effect on time, cost and other work. Offer options, such as swapping it with another item, moving the date, or adding it later. Get a decision from someone with authority and write it down. Small changes may need only a quick note, while large ones need formal agreement.
simple_explain: Write down each new ask, show what it costs, and get a clear decision.
analogy: A change request is a toll gate where new work is weighed before entering the road.
formula_rule: Record, assess, offer options, decide, update the plan.
example_1: A customer asks for an extra report after kick-off.
example_2: Swapping a low-value feature for a new request.
example_3: Moving a go-live date to include extra security checks.
related: scoping, estimating, saying no, decision log
word_family: change, impact, approval, trade-off, baseline
where_used: Delivery | Contracts | Status meetings
mini_conversation: A: Can you add this by next week? | B: We can, if we swap it with another item or move the date.
misconception: ✗ Saying yes to every change keeps customers happy → ✓ Unmanaged changes often lead to late, poor delivery
contrast: Change request vs bug: new wanted behaviour versus a mistake against the agreement.
avoid: Do not accept scope changes in hallway conversations.
memory_trick: Record it, price it, decide it, update it.
real_project: A written trade-off kept an on-time launch when a late request arrived.
real_interview: An interviewer may ask: How do you manage scope changes?
real_daily: It is like adding a stop to a trip and checking the time and fuel.
mcq_question: What should you do with a new request after scope is agreed?
mcq_options: A) Delete the plan B) Accept it secretly C) Assess it and record a decision D) Ignore it
mcq_answer: C
mcq_why: A recorded decision protects the plan and the relationship.
fill_blank: Offering to swap a new item for an old one is a ____ → trade-off
apply: A customer wants an extra report next week without moving the date. What do you offer? → A trade-off, such as swapping another item
explain_task: Explain a simple change request process.
own_task: Draw a one-page change request form with five fields.

@REQ-017
topic: 03 Requirements
level: Intermediate
term: Estimating
cue: How do you give a realistic time or effort for work you have not done yet?
category: Technique
frequency: High
importance: 5
origin: Customers need dates, but early estimates are uncertain, so estimates must be honest about what is known and unknown.
pro_tip: Give a range with the assumptions behind it, and refine as facts arrive.
memory_story: An estimate is a weather forecast, a range with confidence, not a promise.
visual: A bar showing a range from best case to worst case.
emotion: The honesty of admitting uncertainty without losing trust.
definition: Estimating is predicting the effort or time needed for work, usually as a range with stated assumptions, and improving the estimate as more is learned.
explanation: Break the work into small pieces, estimate each, and add time for testing, reviews, waiting on others and surprises. Use past similar work as evidence. State what the estimate assumes and what would change it. Estimates are not commitments, but dates agreed with a customer become expectations, so communicate the uncertainty plainly.
simple_explain: Give a range, say what you assumed, and update it as you learn.
analogy: An estimate is a weather forecast, a range with confidence, not a promise.
formula_rule: Break down, range, assumptions, buffer, refine.
example_1: Two to four days for an integration, assuming test access.
example_2: Adding review and testing time to a build estimate.
example_3: Re-estimating after the first sample of data arrives.
related: scoping, assumptions and risks, change requests, status updates
word_family: estimate, range, buffer, assumption, uncertainty
where_used: Proposals | Planning | Status updates
mini_conversation: A: How long will the integration take? | B: Two to four days, if test access arrives by Monday.
misconception: ✗ An estimate is a promise → ✓ It is a forecast with stated assumptions
contrast: Estimate vs commitment: a forecast versus an agreed delivery date.
avoid: Do not give a single number without assumptions.
memory_trick: Give a range, state the assumptions, refine.
real_project: A range with clear assumptions kept trust when test access arrived late.
real_interview: An interviewer may ask: How do you estimate unfamiliar work?
real_daily: It is like forecasting a journey time with traffic.
mcq_question: What makes an estimate more honest?
mcq_options: A) A range with assumptions B) A promise C) One exact number D) A secret date
mcq_answer: A
mcq_why: It shows uncertainty and what could change the answer.
fill_blank: Extra time added for surprises is called a ____ → buffer
apply: A customer asks for a date on unfamiliar work. What do you give? → A range with assumptions
explain_task: Explain how to give an honest estimate.
own_task: Estimate a small task as a range, and list its three assumptions.

topic: 04 Documentation
level: Beginner
term: README
cue: Which file tells a newcomer what a project is and how to start?
category: Document
frequency: Very high
importance: 5
origin: People arrive at a repository without context, so a front-door file explains what it is, how to run it and where to get help.
pro_tip: Test the README by asking a colleague to follow it without your help.
memory_story: A README is the front door sign and the welcome mat of a project.
visual: A file with sections for what, setup, usage and contacts.
emotion: The welcome of clear first steps.
definition: A README is the first file a reader sees in a project, explaining its purpose, how to install and run it, how to use it, and who to contact.
explanation: A good README starts with one clear sentence on purpose, then gives setup steps in order, a small usage example, configuration notes without secrets, and links to deeper documents. Keep it short and current. Out-of-date setup steps are worse than none. Update it whenever setup changes, and treat it as part of the code review.
simple_explain: It is the first page that tells people what the project is and how to run it.
analogy: A README is the front door sign and the welcome mat of a project.
formula_rule: What, setup, usage, config, contacts.
example_1: A one-line purpose at the top.
example_2: Numbered setup steps that work on a fresh machine.
example_3: A link to the runbook for operations.
related: technical spec, runbook, handover document, decision log
word_family: README, setup, usage, onboarding, markdown
where_used: Repositories | Customer handover | Onboarding
mini_conversation: A: How do I run this? | B: Follow the README, and tell me if any step fails.
misconception: ✗ Documentation can wait until the end → ✓ A short README helps from the first day
contrast: README vs technical spec: how to start versus how it is designed.
avoid: Do not put passwords or keys in a README.
memory_trick: Purpose first, steps next, secrets never.
real_project: A tested README let a customer's engineer run the integration without a call.
real_interview: An interviewer may ask: What belongs in a good README?
real_daily: It is like the instructions on the box of a flat-pack cabinet.
mcq_question: What should a README never contain?
mcq_options: A) Contacts B) Setup steps C) Secrets and keys D) A purpose line
mcq_answer: C
mcq_why: Files are shared and stored widely, so secrets must stay out.
fill_blank: A good README begins with the project's ____ → purpose
apply: A new engineer cannot start the project. Which file should have helped? → The README
explain_task: Explain what a good README contains.
own_task: Write a README outline of six headings for a small integration.

term: Technical spec
cue: Which document explains how a solution will be built and why?
category: Document
frequency: High
importance: 4
origin: Before building, teams need a shared plan that others can review, so mistakes are caught on paper instead of in code.
pro_tip: Keep it short and focus on decisions, interfaces and risks, not every detail.

explanation: A spec lets reviewers challenge the design before effort is spent. It should name what is out of scope, show how components connect, and state how the result will be tested. Alternatives and the reason for the choice help future readers. Keep it versioned and update it when the design changes, or label it as the original plan.

formula_rule: Goal, design, interfaces, alternatives, risks, tests.

word_family: spec, design, interface, alternative, review
where_used: Design reviews | Integrations | Onboarding
mini_conversation: A: Should we start coding now? | B: Share a short spec first so the customer's engineers can check it.
misconception: ✗ A spec must be long to be useful → ✓ A short, clear spec is easier to review
contrast: Spec vs README: a design plan versus a how to run guide.
avoid: Do not leave a spec out of date without saying so.
memory_trick: Plan on paper, review, then build.

apply: You plan a custom integration. What do you write before coding? → A short technical spec

topic: 04 Documentation
level: Intermediate
term: Runbook
cue: Which document tells someone exactly what to do when something goes wrong?
category: Document
frequency: High
importance: 5
origin: Problems often happen at night or when the builder is away, so steps must be written for a person who did not build the system.
pro_tip: Write each step so a tired person at three in the morning could follow it.
memory_story: A runbook is the emergency card in the aeroplane seat pocket.
visual: A numbered page of symptoms, checks and fixes.
emotion: The calm of knowing what to do under pressure.
definition: A runbook is a step-by-step guide for operating a system and handling known problems, listing symptoms, checks, fixes, escalation contacts and how to confirm recovery.
explanation: Organise by symptom, such as job did not run, then give the checks in order, the fix, and how to confirm it worked. Include who to contact if the steps fail and where logs are. Keep commands copy-ready, and test the runbook in a calm moment. Review it after every incident, and mark owners and last-reviewed dates.
simple_explain: Write the exact steps to follow when the system has a known problem.
analogy: A runbook is the emergency card in the aeroplane seat pocket.
formula_rule: Symptom, checks, fix, confirm, escalate.
example_1: Steps to restart a failed nightly job.
example_2: How to check where logs are for an integration.
example_3: Who to call if the fix does not work.
related: README, handover document, postmortems, five whys
word_family: runbook, symptom, escalation, recovery, owner
where_used: Operations | Handover | On-call
mini_conversation: A: The nightly job failed again. | B: Check the runbook entry for it before you escalate.
misconception: ✗ Only the builder needs to know how to fix things → ✓ Others must be able to recover the system from written steps
contrast: Runbook vs README: operating and fixing versus setup and use.
avoid: Do not write steps that need hidden knowledge.
memory_trick: Symptom, check, fix, confirm, escalate.
real_project: A runbook let the customer's team restart a failed job without calling anyone.

mcq_why: People start from what they see going wrong.
fill_blank: The step that checks that a fix really worked is the ____ step → confirm
apply: A job fails at night and the builder is away. What helps the on-call person? → A runbook

category: Document
frequency: High
importance: 4
origin: Months later nobody remembers why a choice was made, so decisions are written down with their reasons.

This avoids repeating old debates and helps new team members. Share the log with the customer where decisions involve them, so there is agreement on what was chosen.
simple_explain: Write down what was decided, why, and by whom.

word_family: decision, rationale, superseded, owner, history
where_used: Projects | Handover | Audits
mini_conversation: A: Why did we choose the nightly import? | B: It is in the decision log with the alternatives.
misconception: ✗ Decisions are obvious and need no record → ✓ They are forgotten or disputed without a record
contrast: Decision log vs meeting notes: final choices versus a record of discussion.
avoid: Do not delete old decisions when they change.
memory_trick: Log the choice and the reason.

term: Meeting notes
cue: How do you turn a meeting into a useful written record?
category: Document
frequency: Very high
importance: 4

explanation: Capture decisions and actions first, then key points and open questions. Use names and dates for every action. Send the notes soon after, and invite corrections. Do not record sensitive remarks that do not belong in a shared document. For customer meetings, notes also serve as confirmation of scope and expectations.
simple_explain: Write down decisions and actions, with names and dates, and send them quickly.
analogy: Meeting notes are the receipt that proves what was agreed.
formula_rule: Attendees, decisions, actions with owner and date, open questions.
example_1: A list of actions with names and due dates.
example_2: A decision to use a nightly import.
example_3: An open question about data access.
related: decision log, status updates, handover document, expectation setting
word_family: notes, action, owner, decision, follow-up
where_used: Customer meetings | Project reviews | Workshops
mini_conversation: A: What did we agree on the call? | B: It is in the notes I sent yesterday.
misconception: ✗ Everyone remembers the same decisions → ✓ People remember meetings differently without a record
contrast: Notes vs decision log: a record of one meeting versus a permanent list of decisions.
avoid: Do not write notes that name no owner for an action.
memory_trick: Decisions first, then actions, owners and dates.
real_project: Prompt notes with named owners got a stuck data request moving within days.
real_interview: An interviewer may ask: How do you follow up after a customer meeting?
