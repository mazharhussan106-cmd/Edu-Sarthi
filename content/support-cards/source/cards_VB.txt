channel: Voice
level: Basic
cefr: A2
kind: Say it
call_stage: Opening
sentence: Thank you for calling [Company]. This is Priya. How can I help you today?
speaker: Agent
situation: The phone rings and you pick up the first call of your shift; you know nothing yet about the caller.
recall_cue: A new call connects. Which three things do you say in your first breath, and in what order?
stress: THANK-you-for-CALL-ing-[COM-pa-ny] · this-is-PRI-ya · how-can-i-HELP-you-to-DAY
linking: thank_you → "thang-kyoo" · this_is → "thi-siz" · help_you → "hel-pyoo"
intonation: Warm and steady on the greeting, with a short pause after the company name and again after your name. "How can I help you today?" is a wh- question, so let it fall gently at the end; do not rush it.
indian_pron_tip: "Thank" and "this" both start with "th". For "thank", put the tongue tip lightly between the teeth and blow (not "tank"); for "this", use the same tongue position with voice (not "dis"). Minimal pairs: thin / tin (for "thank"), then / den (for "this").
register: Neutral
tone: Warm, welcoming, ready
importance: 5
customer_emotion: Hopeful but unsure whether they have reached the right place.
memory_hook: Think of opening a shop door: you thank them for coming in, show your name badge, then ask how you can help.
hindi_meaning: [Company] को कॉल करने के लिए धन्यवाद। मेरा नाम प्रिया है। आज मैं आपकी क्या मदद कर सकता/सकती हूँ?
hindi_roman: [Company] ko call karne ke liye dhanyavaad. Mera naam Priya hai. Aaj main aapki kya madad kar sakta/sakti hoon?
simple_meaning: Thanks for phoning this company. My name is Priya. What would you like help with?
why_it_works: In one breath the caller learns they reached the right company, hears a real person's name, and is invited to explain. "How can I help" assumes you will help, which sounds far more willing than "What is your problem?"
key_chunk: How can I help you ___?
grammar_pattern: Thank you for + verb-ing · This is + name · How can I + base verb …?
grammar_note: After "Thank you for", use the -ing form: "for calling", not "for call".
tone_note: Saying your name makes you a person, not a machine. A flat, fast "Hello, tell me" sounds like you want the call to end quickly.
watch_out: ✗ Thank you for calling to [Company]. I am Priya. How I can help you? → ✓ Thank you for calling [Company]. This is Priya. How can I help you today?
dont_say: "Hello, tell me." — it skips the company name and your name, and sounds like an order.
positive_swap: Instead of "Yes, tell me your problem", say "How can I help you today?"
alt_1: Good morning, you're through to [Company]. My name is Priya. How can I help?
alt_2: Hi, thanks for calling [Company]. Priya speaking. What can I do for you today?
more_formal: Good afternoon, and thank you for calling [Company]. My name is Priya. How may I assist you today?
ask_next: Sure, I can look into that. Could I start with your name, please?
replies_you_may_hear: Hi, I'm calling about my order. · Is this the billing team? · My internet has stopped working.
if_customer_pushes: Customer: "Is this a real person? I've been waiting ages." → Agent: "Yes, I'm Priya, a real person, and I'm sorry about the wait. How can I help you today?"
cultural_note: Many Indian agents say "Myself Priya" or open with "Yes, tell me". International callers may not expect these; they expect "This is Priya" or "My name is Priya", with the company name first.
channel_note: Chat: "Hi, I'm Priya from [Company]. How can I help today?" Email: greet the customer by name and get to the point; no phone-style opening.
when_not_to_use: When you call the customer back, do not say "Thank you for calling"; say "Hi, this is Priya from [Company], returning your call."
mini_conversation: Customer: Hello? | Agent: Thank you for calling [Company]. This is Priya. How can I help you today? | Customer: Hi, my parcel hasn't arrived. | Agent: I'm sorry to hear that. Could I have your order number, please?
source_rule: Open with thanks, the company name and an offer of help, and give your own name.
fill_blank: Thank you ____ calling [Company]. This is Priya. How can I help you today?
fill_answer: for
mcq_question: A call has just connected. Which opening is the most welcoming and complete?
mcq_options: A) Good morning, [Company] customer service. Yes, please tell me your problem. | B) Hello, [Company] customer service, Priya here. What is the problem today? | C) Thank you for calling [Company]. This is Priya. How can I help you today? | D) Hello, this is [Company]. Please give me your name and order number.
mcq_answer: C
mcq_why: C thanks the caller and gives the company, your name and an offer of help. A and B ask for "the problem" bluntly, and D starts with a demand for details and skips your name.
reorder_task: help / today / I / can / How / you
reorder_answer: How can I help you today

hindi_to_english_answer: Thank you for calling [Company]. This is Rahul. How can I help you today?

intonation: A polite request shaped like a yes/no question, so rise slightly on "please?". Slow down on "full name" so it is heard clearly.
indian_pron_tip: "Have" ends in a real /v/: top teeth touch the lower lip, not "haw" or "hab". Practise the pair vest / west so "have" does not slide into a w-sound.
register: Neutral
tone: Polite, simple request
importance: 5

memory_hook: Imagine holding out an open hand for a business card: "Could I have…" asks for it, it does not grab.

hindi_roman: Kya mujhe aapka poora naam mil sakta hai?
simple_meaning: Please tell me your first name and your surname.
why_it_works: "Could I have" is a soft, standard request that international callers hear every day. Asking for the full name up front saves a second question later when two accounts share a first name.
key_chunk: Could I have your ___, please?
grammar_pattern: Could + I + have + your + noun, please?
grammar_note: "Could I have…?" asks to receive something; it is softer than "Give me…" or "Tell me…".
tone_note: The question form and "please" make it polite. "Name?" or "Tell your name" sounds like a form being filled in, not a person helping.
watch_out: ✗ Can you tell your full name? → ✓ Could I have your full name, please?
dont_say: "Tell me your good name." — "good name" echoes Hindi "shubh naam"; international callers may find it odd, and it sounds like an order.
positive_swap: Instead of "Tell me your name", say "Could I have your full name, please?"
alt_1: Can I take your name, please?
alt_2: Who am I speaking with today?
more_formal: Before we go further, could you give me your full name as it appears on the account?
ask_next: Thank you, Ms Okafor. Could you spell your surname for me, please?
replies_you_may_hear: It's James Walker. · Sarah, Sarah Jones. · Why do you need my name?
if_customer_pushes: Customer: "Why do you need my name? Just fix it." → Agent: "I understand you want this sorted quickly. I need your name to find your account, so I can see exactly what happened."
cultural_note: "Your good name" and "May I know your name?" are common Indian English forms. International callers may not expect them; switch to "Could I have your name?" or "Who am I speaking with?"
channel_note: Chat: "Could I have your full name, please?" works as is. Email: the name is usually in the signature, so do not ask; use it in your greeting.
when_not_to_use: Do not ask again if the caller already gave their name in the first sentence; use it instead: "Thanks, Mr Walker."
mini_conversation: Customer: I need help with a refund. | Agent: Sure. Could I have your full name, please? | Customer: It's Anna Brooks. | Agent: Thank you, Ms Brooks. Let me find your account.
source_rule: Verification: ask only for what you need, and state the purpose when the caller asks why.

mcq_answer: B
mcq_why: B is a soft, standard request. A and C are orders, and D sets a condition instead of asking.

reorder_answer: Could I have your full name please
