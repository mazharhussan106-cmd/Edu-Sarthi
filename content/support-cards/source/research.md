# Research brief: English for customer support (voice, chat, email), for Indian agents

Purpose: factual input for 180 flashcards (Basic / Intermediate / Advanced). No cards here.

How to read the tags:
- **[Evidence]**: a study, a dictionary or a framework document.
- **[Industry]**: published practice guidance from a vendor or trade body (Zendesk, Intercom, SQM, ICMI and others). It is widely followed but rarely tested.
- **[Opinion]**: a blog, trainer or columnist view.

Every numbered claim names its source. Where a number could not be traced to a primary source, the brief says so and the number should not go on a card.

Method note: page fetches were blocked in this environment, so the claims come from search-engine summaries of the linked pages. Before putting any exact quotation on a card, open the URL and check the wording.

## 1. Voice-call etiquette

### Opening and greeting
- The usual pattern is thanks, company name, then an offer of help: "Thank you for calling [Company]… How can I help you today?" [Industry/teaching] [source](https://learnenglish.life/guides/english-for-customer-service/)
- Typical courtesy openers and closers for phone English are "Good morning", "Thank you for your time" and "Have a great day". [Teaching material; provenance unclear] [source](https://web1svr.unicron.com/Download_PDFS/mL4B05/602358/Telephoning%20In%20English%20Cambridge.pdf)
- Useful phrases for checking names and repeats: "Who's calling, please?", asking the caller to spell a name, and "I'm sorry, could you please repeat that?" [Teaching] [source](https://www.fluentu.com/blog/english/business-english-telephone-phrases/)

### Identity verification
- Sample wording seen in scripts: "For security purposes, can you please confirm…" [Industry sample, not a standard] [source](https://convin.ai/blog/customer-verification-call-center)
- Frame the request around the customer's benefit: "For verification purposes, may I have…?" or "To protect the privacy of your account, may I have…?" Verification is presented as being *for the customer's security*. [Industry] [source](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/call-handling-guidelines-238682174/238682174)
- How much to verify depends on how much account access the caller needs. Identifying the caller by phone number first can shorten calls. [Industry, vendor] [source](https://poly.ai/blog/automating-identity-verification-in-the-contact-center-what-you-should-know)
- No authoritative source says one exact wording is best. The common pattern is to state the purpose and ask only for what is needed. [Gap]

### Putting a caller on hold
- **Ask permission first.** Callers who agree to wait are described as more patient than callers who are put on hold without being asked. [Industry] [source](https://www.anser.com/qa/hold) This page also claims callers who are asked perceive the hold as about 30% shorter. **No study is cited, so do not use the number.**
- **Say why and give a time estimate**, not "a moment". [Industry] [source](https://www.anser.com/qa/hold), [source](https://melotel.com/what-are-the-right-and-wrong-ways-to-place-callers-on-hold/). Replace "a moment" with an estimate and offer a callback, for example "up to two minutes, or I can call you back". [Industry] [source](https://exotel.com/blog/bad-call-center-phrases-15-to-avoid/)
- **Never hold without telling the caller**, and do not use mute in place of hold, because callers can overhear internal talk. [Industry] [source](https://melotel.com/what-are-the-right-and-wrong-ways-to-place-callers-on-hold/), [source](https://vida.io/blog/how-to-put-someone-on-hold)
- **Thank the caller first when you return**, before giving information. [Industry] [source](https://vida.io/blog/how-to-put-someone-on-hold)
- Practice material on holds and dead air: [source](https://www.verequest.com/courses/holds-dead-air-and-transfers)
- When offering a callback, state the expected wait first. Setting a wait-time expectation raises callback acceptance. [Industry, vendor documentation] [source](https://docs.medallia.com/mindful/mindful-callback/faqs/best-practices/quote-wait-time-before-offering-callbacks)

### Transferring
- **Warm transfer** (also called attended or consultative): the first agent briefs the receiving agent before connecting the caller, so the caller does not have to repeat the problem. Use it for complex, emotional or sensitive issues. [Industry] [source](https://www.calilio.com/blogs/warm-transfer-vs-cold-transfer), [source](https://krispcall.com/customer-experience/cold-transfer-vs-warm-transfer/)
- **Cold transfer** (also called blind): the call is passed on with no briefing. It is faster, but the caller may have to repeat themselves or be misrouted. It is acceptable for a simple reroute. [Industry] [source](https://krispcall.com/customer-experience/cold-transfer-vs-warm-transfer/), [source](https://www.calilio.com/blogs/warm-transfer-vs-cold-transfer)
- A typical warm-transfer sequence: ask the caller's permission, explain the short hold, introduce the caller and the issue to the colleague, introduce the colleague to the caller, then leave the call. [Industry, a company's documented procedure] [source](https://desk.zoho.com/portal/cinchhomeservices/en/kb/articles/call-transfer-procedures)
- Even when handing off, the first agent stays accountable until the customer reaches the right place. [Opinion] [source](https://www.entrepreneur.com/article/227458)
- A vendor blog cites "Forrester" figures for cold transfers (38% lower satisfaction, 67% more repeat calls). **These could not be traced to Forrester, so do not use them.**

### Active listening and paraphrasing
- Paraphrasing catches misunderstandings early and lets the customer correct you. Sample opener: "Let me make sure I got everything." Short verbal signals on calls: "I see", "That makes sense". [Industry] [source](https://www.quo.com/blog/active-listening-in-customer-service/)
- Confirmation form: "If I understand correctly, you're experiencing [issue]. Is that right?" [Industry/opinion] [source](https://customerthink.com/?p=1049518)
- Interrupting frustrates customers. Take notes instead of cutting in. [Industry] [source](https://www.cxtoday.com/contact-centre/valuable-active-listening-exercises-for-contact-center-agents)
- Listening well enough to paraphrase saves time overall. [Opinion] [source](https://www.capterra.com/resources/paraphrasing-customer-service-success-secret-weapon/)
- A vendor claims a 12% CSAT rise from paraphrasing. **This is unverified, so do not use it.** [source](https://fonolo.com/?p=53552)

### Empathy statements
- "I can see how that would be frustrating" is the model empathy line in the HEARD framework. [Industry] [source](https://pollackpeacebuilding.com/blog/heard-method/)
- Instead of "sorry for the inconvenience", acknowledge the feeling ("I understand your frustration") and pair it with a fix. [Industry, vendor] [source](https://www.livechat.com/success/sorry-for-the-inconvenience), [source](https://www.bolddesk.com/blogs/sorry-for-the-inconvenience)

### Apologising well
- In a peer-reviewed study (Lewicki, Polin & Lount, 2016, *Negotiation and Conflict Management Research*; two experiments, 755 participants), an apology could contain six parts: regret, explanation, **acknowledgement of responsibility**, repentance, **offer of repair** and request for forgiveness. Acknowledging responsibility ranked most important and offering repair ranked second. Request for forgiveness ranked least important. More parts made an apology more effective. [Evidence] [source](https://news.osu.edu/the-6-elements-of-an-effective-apology-according-to-science/), [source](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/effective-apologies-include-six-elements.html)
- In HEARD, the apology is made on behalf of the company even when the agent did not cause the problem. [Industry] [source](https://pollackpeacebuilding.com/blog/heard-method/)
- "Sorry for the inconvenience" is criticised as vague and scripted. [Opinion, vendor blogs] [source](https://www.livechat.com/success/sorry-for-the-inconvenience)

### Ownership language and setting expectations
- "I hope we can fix this soon" is weak. A commitment is stronger: "I'll personally follow up… and call you with an update by [time]". [Industry] [source](https://entripy-82.helpscoutdocs.com/article/392-take-owership-maintain-control-of-the-call)
- Replace vague promises with a clear timeline, a ticket number and the next step. [Industry] [source](https://exotel.com/blog/bad-call-center-phrases-15-to-avoid/), [source](https://www.quo.com/blog/customer-service-expectations/)
- Unclear language causes service failures. Be specific about what will happen and when. [Opinion, Jeff Toister] [source](https://toistersolutions.com/blog/2016/7/6/how-clear-language-can-help-you-avoid-service-failures)
- Customer-effort research (CEB/Gartner, published in HBR in 2010 and based on more than 75,000 interactions) found that reducing customer effort predicts loyalty better than "delighting" the customer. [Evidence, secondary summary] [source](https://www.renascence.io/journal/gartners-customer-effort-research-why-effort-beats-delight). Gartner: customers with a low-effort resolution are 61% more likely to stay. [Evidence, Gartner press release] [source](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2020-08-19-gartner-reveals-two-actions-customer-service-leaders-)

### Closing the call
- Summarise the key points and next steps, and use a consistent resolution-check question at the end. [Industry, SQM Group] [source](https://sqmgroup.com/resources/library/blog/5-pros-and-cons-agents-asking-customers-if-they-resolved-issues)
- SQM notes that "Is there anything else I can do for you?" **assumes the issue is already resolved**, which may not be true. Ask whether the issue is resolved first. Keep a separate closing for calls that are not resolved, which tells the customer the options and next steps. [Industry] [same source](https://sqmgroup.com/resources/library/blog/5-pros-and-cons-agents-asking-customers-if-they-resolved-issues)

## 2. Positive language ("positive scripting")

- Replace limitation words ("can't", "unfortunately") with solution words ("can", "help", "here's what I can do"). Shopify's example turns "you can't keep the item" into an apology, a replacement and a free return label. [Industry] [source](https://www.shopify.com/blog/positive-scripting)
- **"Calm down"** is like "adding fuel to the fire". Acknowledge the frustration instead. [Industry] [source](https://www.callcentrehelper.com/positive-scripting-examples-for-better-customer-conversations-253958.htm)
- Paired swaps: "Just calm down" becomes "I understand your frustration", and "That's not possible" becomes "Here's what we can do instead". [Industry, vendor] [source](https://quidget.ai/?p=1975)
- **"You have to / you need to"** makes customers bristle. Frame it as a shared process ("We find it works best when you…"). The requirement stays; only the tone changes. [Opinion] [source](https://digitaldealer.com/news/5-things-employees-say-sabotage-customer-service-drive-customers-away/74915/)
- Swap "problem" for softer words ("situation", "issue"). [Opinion] [source](https://customerthink.com/part-3-are-you-using-these-magic-customer-service-words/)
- A list of 15 phrases call-centre agents should never use (vague holds, vague promises and others): [Industry] [source](https://exotel.com/blog/bad-call-center-phrases-15-to-avoid/)
- More positive-language examples: [source](https://www.cxtoday.com/wfo/positive-language-usage-20-examples-that-will-make-every-interaction-a-little-bit-brighter)
- **"Policy says…"**: no reputable source on this exact phrase was found. The related, sourced advice is to explain the *reason* for a decision with empathy and focus on what you can do ([Zapier](https://zapier.com/blog/customer-support-language-and-tone)), and to offer an alternative and follow up ([Hiver](https://hiverhq.com/?p=46426)). [Gap: treat the "policy" card as derived advice]
- **"No problem" vs "You're welcome"**: this is a genuine debate. [Opinion]
  - Critics say "no problem" implies the request *could* have been a problem. Lexicographer Erin McKean noted that many readers dislike it in commercial settings. [source](https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/is-there-a-problem-with-no-problem/), [source](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4093603.html), [source](https://www.td.org/insights/when-you-say-no-problem-you-may-have-customer-service-problem)
  - Defenders say the meaning is harmless and the divide is mostly generational. [source](https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/is-there-a-problem-with-no-problem/), [source](https://waywordradio.org/no-problem-youre-welcome/)
  - Safe teaching line: "You're welcome" is the safer default in formal service. "No problem" is common and acceptable in casual exchanges.
- Over-scripting backfires: customers dislike agents who use pre-set scripts rigidly. [Opinion/industry] [source](https://www.customerthermometer.com/customer-service/excellent-customer-service-phrases/)

## 3. De-escalation frameworks

None of these frameworks has a primary, peer-reviewed source. They are training acronyms passed between companies, and different sources give different letters. Present them as "one common version".

| Acronym | Letters | Attribution / source |
|---|---|---|
| **HEARD** | Hear, Empathize, Apologize, Resolve, Diagnose (find the root cause without blaming anyone) | Usually credited to Disney / the Walt Disney Institute, but the sources are trainers and blogs, not a Disney document. [source](https://pollackpeacebuilding.com/blog/heard-method/), [source](https://www.parcelperform.com/insights/customer-service-disney-tips) |
| **LAST** | Listen, Apologize, Solve, Thank | Hospitality and guest-service training. [source](https://www.xola.com/articles/how-to-use-the-last-method-for-customer-service/), [source](https://centeredgesoftware.com/blog/5-effective-ways-to-resolve-complaints-with-last/) |
| **LEAST** | Listen, Empathize, Apologize, Solve, Thank | A LAST variant. [source](https://readytrainingonline.com/articles/customer-service/) |
| **LATTE** | Listen, Acknowledge, Take action, Thank, Explain | Credited to Starbucks barista training (reported in secondary sources). [source](https://trainingcenter.acgov.org/2020/03/latte-for-customer-service), [source](https://www.pharmacytimes.com/view/how-to-handle-angry-pharmacy-customers) |
| **LEAP** (customer service) | Listen, Empathize, Apologize, Problem-solve | Customer-service training reader. [source](https://rachel.core2learn.org/modules/en-learnsaylor/mod/page/view.php@id=18749.html). Another version is Listen, Empathize, Ask, Produce. [source](https://www.servicetitan.com/blog/pantheon-recap-hearing-is-not-enough) |
| **LEAPS** (security/health) | Listen, Empathize, Ask, Paraphrase, Summarize | Security and healthcare de-escalation. [source](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/ca-ab-abst/communication/leaps-communication-method), [source](https://caringforcare.co.uk/leaps-model/) |
| **A.I.D.** | No source found | **Do not use** unless your client supplies its own definition. |

Common pattern across all of them: listen without interrupting, acknowledge the feeling, apologise or take responsibility, act, thank, and explain what happens next.

## 4. Live chat

- **Speed matters.** Zendesk says satisfaction drops once a chat waits more than about 30 seconds, and long gaps between replies also hurt it. [Industry, Zendesk] [source](https://www.zendesk.com/blog/6-best-practices-chat-etiquette/)
- **Zendesk chat etiquette** [Industry] [source](https://www.zendesk.com/blog/6-best-practices-chat-etiquette/), [source](https://www.zendesk.com/in/blog/messaging-and-chat/live-chat/live-chat-support-skills/):
  - correct grammar and spelling
  - no internet shorthand
  - no jokes
  - no ALL CAPS
  - no excuses for delays
  - transfer to a supervisor if the customer asks
- **Greeting and expectations**: introduce yourself as you would on a call, and state a typical reply time. [Industry] [source](https://www.chaindesk.ai/blog/chat-etiquette-for-customer-service-guide). A sample auto-reply: "We usually respond within X–Y minutes during business hours." [Industry community] [source](https://community.intercom.com/messenger-8/how-do-you-handle-customers-expecting-instant-replies-in-intercom-messenger-14016)
- **Short messages**: keep replies short but answer every question. If the customer sounds urgent, reply briefly and at once. [Industry] [source](https://www.stayntouch.com/wp-content/uploads/intercom-guide.pdf)
- **Name and tone**: Intercom advises a conversational voice over "Dear customer" and using the customer's first name. Customers tend to mirror the tone they receive. [Industry] [source](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/198-our-best-practice-guide-to-customer-support)
- **Canned responses**: use one only if it fully matches the question. If unsure, ask a clarifying question first, and keep it personal. [Industry] [source](https://www.whoson.com/live-chat-best-practice/canned-responses-dos-donts)
- **Emoji** [Evidence, academic]:
  - Agents who used emoticons were seen as warmer but less competent. After a service failure, customers value competence more. [source](https://www.marketing-interactive.com/study-brands-should-beware-using-emoticons-in-customer-communication/), [source](https://theses.lib.polyu.edu.hk/handle/200/12107)
  - Emoji that match the message raised satisfaction in support contexts but not after a failure (Pescatore & Farace, 2025). [source](https://archives.marketing-trends-congress.com/2025/pages/PDF/108.pdf)
  - In formal work email, smileys did not raise warmth and did lower perceived competence. [source](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20200220005312/en)
  - Teaching line: no emoji with an upset customer or a complaint. At most a light, matching emoji in a friendly, informal chat, and only if the brand allows it.
- **Ending chats**: return the customer's goodbye. If the customer goes silent, check in at intervals, warn them, then close. [Industry] [source](https://www.whoson.com/live-chat-best-practice/canned-responses-dos-donts), [source](https://livechat.knowledgebase.ai/canned-response-examples/)
- **When chat is the wrong channel**: if it takes more than two messages to work out what the customer needs, switch to a call. This is Toister's email advice and applies here by extension. [Opinion] [source](https://www.toistersolutions.com/blog/2013/1/1/email-tip-what-do-they-want)

## 5. Email support

- **Subject line**: it must reflect the content and include the case or ticket number when replying. [Industry, Freshworks] [source](https://www.freshworks.com/explore-cx/tips-to-write-a-good-support-email-with-email-templates/). Keep it short, about 4 to 5 words, and do not edit the subject when replying, or threading breaks. [Industry] [source](https://www.keeping.com/content/email-etiquette-customer-service/). Be specific: "Update on your refund request", not "Important information". [Industry] [source](https://salto.io/blog-posts/tips-and-tricks-to-improve-your-zendesk-emails-from-a-zendesk-expert)
- **Greeting**: use the customer's name, not a generic "Dear customer". [Industry, Zendesk and Intercom] [source](https://www.zendesk.com/dk/blog/use-customer-service-email-templates-save-time/), [source](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/198-our-best-practice-guide-to-customer-support)
- **Sign-off**: a Zendesk analysis (quoted secondhand) found "Best regards" and "Cheers" did better than other closings or none, and "Best wishes" did worse. The reasons are unclear, so treat this as weak. [Industry, secondary] [source](https://customerthink.com/?p=113471)
- **Plain language** (ICMI): customers are not experts in your product, so replace or explain jargon. Plain language means "easy to read, understand, and use", not dumbed down. [Industry, trade body] [source](https://icmi.com/resources/2021/plain-language-in-customer-service)
- **Structure**: number multi-step instructions to cut follow-up questions. [Industry] [source](https://salto.io/blog-posts/tips-and-tricks-to-improve-your-zendesk-emails-from-a-zendesk-expert)
- **Saying no**: Help Scout advises declining without the word "no" and setting expectations early, so refusals are rarer. [Industry] [source](https://helpscout.com/blog/how-to-tell-a-customer-no). Explain the reason, show empathy, and lead with what you *can* do. [Industry] [source](https://zapier.com/blog/customer-support-language-and-tone). Offer an alternative, then follow up. [Industry] [source](https://hiverhq.com/?p=46426)
- **Follow-ups**: put "Follow-up" or "Re:" in the subject so the customer knows it is not a new issue. [Industry] [source](https://salto.io/blog-posts/tips-and-tricks-to-improve-your-zendesk-emails-from-a-zendesk-expert)
- **Native vs non-native writers**: a study of 164 customer-service email replies found little difference in structure between firms from native and non-native English-speaking countries. [Evidence] [source](https://www.revistaiberica.org/index.php/iberica/article/view/273)

## 6. Indian English in support: phrases and pronunciation

**Framing first.** Linguists treat these as features of a legitimate variety, Indian English, not as errors. The teaching aim is to *switch* for international customers, not to "correct" Indian English. Cards should say "International customers may not expect this", not "This is wrong".

### Phrases

- **"Do the needful"**
  - Rarely used outside India. One guide suggests "take the necessary action" or "help with this". [Opinion, ESL] [source](https://www.talkdrill.com/blog/spoken-english/common-mistakes/common-english-mistakes-indians/)
  - It is not archaic in Indian English, but it is in North American and British English, where it can sound jocular. OED citations go back to 1681. [Linguist commentary] [source](https://languagehat.com/do-the-needful/)
- **"Revert" meaning "reply"**
  - In general English, "revert" means "return to a previous state". The Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary (8th edition) lists the "reply" sense as Indian English. [Evidence, dictionary, via Ben Zimmer] [source](https://www.vocabulary.com/cm/wordroutes/2307/)
  - "Revert back" is redundant. Use "get back to you". [Opinion, ESL] [source](https://www.talkdrill.com/blog/spoken-english/common-mistakes/common-english-mistakes-indians/)
- **"Prepone"**: the OED has examples from 1913, but in recent decades it has been "mostly restricted to India". Outside India, say "bring forward" or "move earlier". [Evidence, dictionary, via Ben Zimmer] [source](https://www.vocabulary.com/cm/wordroutes/2307/)
- **"Kindly"**: correct but old-fashioned in modern US and UK English. Use "Could you…?" or "Please…". [Opinion, ESL] [source](https://llexi.com/l1-errors/common-english-errors-hindi-speakers.html). Purdue OWL lists "kindly requested" and "aforesaid" as verbose wording that can be simplified. [Academic writing centre] [source](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/multilingual/multilingual_students/writing_for_an_indian_business_audience/index.html)
- **"Please find the same attached"**: name the thing instead ("I've attached your invoice."). [Opinion, ESL] [source](https://www.talkdrill.com/blog/workplace-english/emails/email-etiquette-mistakes-indian-professionals/)
- **"Out of station"**: say "out of town". [Opinion, ESL] [source](https://llexi.com/l1-errors/common-english-errors-hindi-speakers.html)
- **"Do the needful" plus "kindly revert" together**: one guide says this combination signals weak English to international readers. [Opinion, commercial] [source](https://www.talkdrill.com/blog/workplace-english/emails/email-etiquette-mistakes-indian-professionals/)
- **"I am having" with stative verbs** ("I am having two accounts", "I am knowing"):
  - Corpus studies confirm Indian English uses the progressive with stative verbs (*have*, *know*) more than British English does. This is linked to influence from Indian first languages. [Evidence] [source](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/04b6bd76-5d17-4e6a-a0b9-1e1d98d19930/full), [source](https://riuma.uma.es/xmlui/handle/10630/11233), [source](https://repository.nwu.ac.za/handle/10394/15268)
  - International customers expect "I have" and "I know".
- **"May I know…?"**: no source found. It is grammatical. "Could you tell me…?" or "Could I have…?" are the more common international forms. [Gap: brief writer's suggestion only]
- **Emphatic "itself" / "only"** ("I will do it today itself", "He is like that only"): no reliable source found in this search. It is widely discussed as an Indian English feature, but **mark it as unsourced**. [Gap]
- **Call-centre evidence**: a thesis studies which accent features cause US callers difficulty with Indian agents, and notes that some US firms brought outsourced support back home after customers complained about communication breakdowns. [Evidence, thesis] [source](https://repository.stcloudstate.edu/engl_etds/148)

### Pronunciation

- **v / w**: in Indian English, /v/ and /w/ are widely described as merged into a single sound, a labiodental approximant /ʋ/. Sailaja argues the merger is complete only in non-standard varieties, and acoustic evidence is rare. [Evidence, ICPhS 2019] [source](https://assta.org/proceedings/ICPhS2019/papers/ICPhS%5F1431.pdf). Practical fix: for V, the top teeth touch the lower lip; for W, round the lips with no teeth contact. [Opinion, commercial] [source](https://www.talkdrill.com/blog/pronunciation/indian-english/indian-english-pronunciation/)
- **th**: /θ/ (think) and /ð/ (this) do not exist in the major Indian languages, so they are often said as t/d ("tink", "dis"). [Opinion, commercial; consistent with standard descriptions] [source](https://www.talkdrill.com/blog/pronunciation/indian-english/indian-english-pronunciation/)
- **Retroflex t/d**: English t/d are alveolar. Hindi speakers often use the retroflex position instead, which makes words like "doctor" and "letter" sound heavier. This varies by first language. [Opinion, commercial] [source](https://www.talkdrill.com/blog/pronunciation/indian-english/indian-english-pronunciation/)
- **Rhythm**: Indian English is generally considered syllable-timed (Gargesh 2008), but Pingali (2009) and Fuchs (2012) question this. A 2017 interactional study found it *tends towards* syllable timing compared with American English. [Evidence, contested] [source](https://assta.org/proceedings/ICPhS2019/papers/ICPhS%5F1431.pdf), [source](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01498953), [source](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/veaw.g68.07fuc/html)
- **Word stress**: stress is usually on the same syllable as in British or American English, but the strong/weak contrast is realised differently (Fuchs & Maxwell). In a perception study, Indian English listeners identified iambic stress (ob-JECT) at about 54% accuracy. [Evidence] [source](https://iaak.uni-bonn.de/bael/en/research/word-stress-perception-in-indian-english), [source](https://cris-portal.uni-muenster.de/portal/en/publication/20007334), [source](https://assta.org/proceedings/ICPhS2019Microsite/pdf/full-paper_606.pdf). Teaching point: noun/verb pairs such as REcord/reCORD and OBject/obJECT need practice.
- **Final consonants and clusters**: final consonant clusters are often simplified, and final consonants can lose voicing (Wiltshire 2005; Khan & Cheshire 1991). Some speakers add a vowel before s-clusters ("iskul" for school). That example is documented for Bengali speakers. [Evidence] [source](https://slam.lin.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/106/2020/01/2005-Wiltshire-EWW.pdf), [source](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/english-around-the-world/final-consonant-cluster-simplification-in-a-variety-of-indian-english/AB78E80B5EDE29BBD8B2A6A8D30EC9E4), [source](https://journals.uvic.ca/index.php/WPLC/article/view/5670/2196)
- **Intelligibility**:
  - Speaking rate, intonation and stress (suprasegmentals) and individual sounds (segmentals) all affected how well listeners from 16 English varieties understood Indian English speakers. [Evidence, Nottingham dissertation] [source](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/31046/)
  - Listeners understand familiar accents better. [Evidence, Interspeech 2009] [source](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2009/weber09_interspeech.html)

## 7. CEFR and customer-service English

- The CEFR Companion Volume (Council of Europe, 2020) added new scales, including **"Using telecommunications"**, plus mediation scales and "plus" levels (A2+, B1+, B2+). [Evidence] [source](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4), [source](https://www.eaquals.org/2018/06/20/the-cefr-companion-volume-launch-conference-may-2018-tim-goodier/)
- **Using telecommunications, B1**: "Can use telecommunications for routine messages (e.g. arrangements for a meeting) and to obtain basic services (e.g. book a hotel room or make a medical appointment)." [Evidence, quoted in a publisher's CEFR mapping] [source](https://edit.hueber.de/media/36/Language%20Hub_Elementary_SB_CEFR_Mapping.pdf)
- **Obtaining goods and services, B2**: negotiate a solution to a dispute, explain a problem and make clear that the provider must make a concession, and argue for compensation using persuasive language. [Evidence, scale reproduced by Macmillan] [source](https://api.macmillanenglish.com/fileadmin/user_upload/Blog_and_Resources/Blogs_and_articles/CEFR-all-scales-and-all-skills.pdf)
- **B1 spoken interaction**: exchange, check and confirm information, deal with less routine situations, and explain why something is a problem. [Evidence, secondary checklist] [source](https://www.oebv.at/internal/f28d2222-b4fc-4803-aac5-79bd48ff8b8a)
- The official descriptor bank is the Council of Europe's search tool. **Check exact A2 and C1 wording there before printing it.** [source](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/cefr-descriptors-search)
- **Practical mapping for this deck** (brief writer's synthesis from the descriptors above, not an official CEFR statement):
  - **A2 (Basic)**: fixed formulas: greeting, "Could you spell that, please?", "One moment, please", thanks and goodbye.
  - **B1 (Intermediate)**: routine calls; check and confirm information; explain a simple problem; hold and transfer politely; standard emails.
  - **B2 (Intermediate/Advanced)**: handle complaints and disputes, explain a refusal with reasons and alternatives, negotiate a solution, paraphrase.
  - **C1 (Advanced)**: control tone and register precisely, de-escalate, adapt to the customer's style, write concise "no" emails that keep the relationship.
- Teaching material at B1 and B2 uses "I'm afraid…", "I'm not sure, but…" and "Let me direct you to…". [Teaching] [source](https://eslbrains.com/esl-lesson-on-customer-service/)

## 8. Phone tone

- **Smiling is audible.** In Drahota, Costall & Reddy (2007, *Speech Communication*), listeners told smiling from non-smiling speech above chance, even without seeing the speaker. They also told some smile types apart. [Evidence] [source](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/01/080111224745.htm), [source](https://www.npr.org/2008/01/19/18255131/hearing-a-smile-in-tone-of-voice). The study did not test phone calls specifically, so "smile on the phone" is an application of the finding.
- **Pace**: speaking rate affects how well Indian English speakers are understood. [Evidence] [source](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/31046/). A tutoring blog says Indian English speakers are often heard as "fast talkers" by Americans. [Opinion] [source](https://magoosh.com/toefl/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/2124). **No reputable words-per-minute figure was found, so do not put a WPM number on a card.**
- **Intonation for questions**:
  - Yes/no questions usually rise; wh- questions usually fall. A falling yes/no question can signal that you expect "yes". A rising wh- question asks for repetition. [Teaching/linguistics] [source](https://pronuncian.com/podcasts/episode213), [source](https://cla-acl.artsci.utoronto.ca/pdfs/actes-2010/CLA2010_Hedberg_et_al.pdf)
  - "Would you like A or B?": a fall on the last item presents a choice; a rise makes it a yes/no question. [Teaching] [source](https://campusweb.howardcc.edu/ehicks/YE618/Mastering%20American%20Pronunciation/Intonation/Intonation_print.html)
- **Pauses and dead air**: tell the caller what you are doing during silence, and use hold for longer gaps. [Industry] [source](https://www.verequest.com/courses/holds-dead-air-and-transfers)

## Rules for card writers

1. **Hold:** ask permission, give a reason and a real time ("about two minutes", not "a moment"), and thank the caller first when you return.
2. **Long hold:** check back before the promised time is up, and offer a callback instead of a long wait.
3. **Never** use mute as hold, and never hold without telling the caller.
4. **Transfer:** prefer warm. Tell the caller who you are transferring them to and why, brief the colleague, and introduce the caller and the issue so they do not have to repeat themselves.
5. **Verification:** state the purpose ("To protect your account…") and ask only for what you need.
6. **Paraphrase** before solving: "So, if I've understood correctly, … Is that right?"
7. **Empathy line, then action.** Never an empathy line on its own: "I can see why that's frustrating. Here's what I'll do."
8. **Apologise specifically** and take responsibility, then offer a fix. Replace "Sorry for the inconvenience" with "I'm sorry your order arrived late. I'll…"
9. **Use "I will", plus a time**, instead of "we'll try" or "hopefully soon".
10. **Never say "calm down".** Name the feeling instead.
11. **Swap "can't" for "can":** lead with what is possible ("What I can do is…").
12. **Swap "you have to" for guidance:** "The quickest way is to…" or "Could you…?"
13. **Don't hide behind "policy"**: give the reason in plain words, then an alternative. Mark this card as derived advice.
14. **Thank-you replies:** teach "You're welcome" as the formal default. Mention that "No problem" is a debated choice and fine only in casual exchanges, not that it is "wrong".
15. **Close the call** by summarising the next steps, checking the issue is resolved, then "Is there anything else…?". Don't ask "anything else" on an unresolved call.
16. **Chat:** greet with your name, reply fast, and send short messages. Split long answers into a few lines.
17. **Chat:** no shorthand, no ALL CAPS, no jokes. Use the customer's first name naturally, not in every line.
18. **Chat:** use a canned reply only when it fully fits, and personalise it.
19. **Emoji:** none with complaints or upset customers. At most one matching emoji in a friendly chat, if the brand allows it.
20. **Ending a chat:** return the goodbye. If the customer goes silent, check in, warn, then close politely.
21. **Email subject:** specific, short and with the ticket number. Keep the subject line when replying.
22. **Email body:** greet by name, use plain words, number the steps, and give one clear next step.
23. **Saying no by email:** reason, then empathy, then an alternative, then a follow-up. Avoid a bare "No".
24. **Indian English switches** for international customers:
    - "revert" → "reply" / "get back to you" (never "revert back")
    - "prepone" → "bring forward"
    - "do the needful" → say the actual action
    - "kindly" → "please" / "Could you…?"
    - "the same" → name the thing
    - "out of station" → "out of town"
25. **Frame these as switches, not errors.** Say "International customers may not expect this", since dictionaries record "revert" and "prepone" as Indian English.
26. **Stative verbs:** "I have two accounts", not "I am having". "I understand", not "I am understanding".
27. **Pronunciation cards** need a minimal pair and a mouth instruction: v/w (vest/west, teeth on lip vs rounded lips), th/t (thin/tin), stress pairs (REcord/reCORD).
28. **Pace:** slow down, pause between ideas, and say final consonants clearly (asked, helped, months). Never print a words-per-minute figure.
29. **Question intonation:** rise on yes/no questions and fall on wh- questions. Use a falling end to offer a choice ("Email or SMS?").
30. **No invented numbers.** Use only the statistics in this brief that carry a source (Gartner 61%; Lewicki's ranking), and name the source on the card. Leave out the unverified ones: the 30% shorter hold, the Forrester transfer figures and the 12% paraphrasing figure.
31. **Name frameworks honestly:** "HEARD (often credited to Disney)", "LATTE (credited to Starbucks)". Do not use A.I.D. without a definition from the client.
